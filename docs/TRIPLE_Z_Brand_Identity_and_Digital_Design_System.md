# TRIPLE Z

## Brand Identity & Digital Design System

**Version:** 1.0 --- Strategic Foundation\
**Status:** Direction approved; production specifications require
validation\
**Primary direction:** Premium Contemporary Streetwear × Football
Heritage\
**Creative balance:** Football Editorial (B) + Signature Streetwear (C),
with restraint and polish\
**Document owner:** Triple Z Brand / Digital Team

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 0. How to Use This Document

This is the master specification for Triple Z's brand identity, digital
experience, ecommerce design system, and future brand applications. It
is not a moodboard, a collection of attractive mockups, or a one-off
website brief.

Use it to make consistent decisions across: - Brand identity and visual
language - Ecommerce website and future digital products - Product
presentation and product-detail pages - Collection launches and campaign
art direction - Packaging, labels, hangtags, and printed collateral -
Social media and paid advertising - Motion design and interactive
experiences - Future product categories and international expansion

**Important status distinction:** This document defines the strategic
direction, operating principles, architecture, and implementation
requirements. Any color values, font choices, logo variants,
measurements, or visual examples marked *provisional* must be validated
against the supplied logo artwork, actual products, production methods,
licensing requirements, and accessibility tests before they are treated
as final brand standards.

## 0.1 Decision labels

-   **Approved direction:** Explicitly aligned with the current brief.
-   **Proposed standard:** Recommended system rule pending design
    review.
-   **Provisional:** A hypothesis to test, not a final brand decision.
-   **Production gate:** Must be verified before release.

## 0.2 Core brief

Triple Z is intended to be an independent premium fashion / streetwear
brand whose creative world is informed by football culture. The product
should feel stylish enough for everyday wear, while its relationship to
football remains meaningful and distinctive. Football is a source of
culture, stories, codes, and inspiration---not a limitation that forces
every product to look like conventional supporter merchandise.

The current logo and product visuals are starting material, not a
requirement to preserve every existing visual treatment. The design
system must support future collections and new products without
rebuilding the brand each time.

The business has been described by the owner as operating at substantial
commercial scale, including a stated monthly sales figure of EGP 30
million. This figure is treated here as owner-provided context, not
independently audited financial data. The design system should therefore
be designed for operational maturity, consistency, measurable ecommerce
outcomes, and future scale---not as a starter-shop template.

# 1. Brand Strategy

## 1.1 Brand ambition

Build Triple Z into a recognizable contemporary fashion brand with a
distinct visual signature, strong product storytelling, and a coherent
customer experience across every touchpoint.

The ambition is not simply to look expensive. The ambition is to make
the product, the presentation, the digital experience, and the brand's
behavior consistently justify customer trust and perceived value.

## 1.2 Strategic positioning

**Working positioning statement**

> Triple Z creates contemporary streetwear shaped by the emotion, visual
> codes, and cultural history of football---translated into wearable,
> design-led fashion.

This is an internal positioning statement, not necessarily
consumer-facing copy.

## 1.3 Brand pillars

### 1. Contemporary style

Products should work as clothing first: proportion, fit, color,
material, and styling must feel intentional even to someone who does not
recognize the football reference.

### 2. Football heritage

Football references should carry meaning. Use player eras, national
colors, iconic numbers, kit construction, supporter culture, archival
typography, and match-day rituals thoughtfully. Avoid reducing every
concept to a large player name or copied team crest.

### 3. Signature design

Triple Z needs ownable design codes: a considered use of the three-Z
motif, a recognizable typographic approach, repeatable graphic
structures, and distinctive product details. These codes should be
recognizable without forcing the full logo onto every surface.

### 4. Product integrity

The perceived value of the brand must be supported by real product
quality, accurate imagery, transparent specifications, consistent
finishing, and honest product information.

### 5. Editorial confidence

Communication should feel confident, culturally aware, and visually
composed. Avoid unnecessary effects, excessive gradients, random
decorative elements, and generic "luxury" clichés.

## 1.5 Brand principles

1.  Product before decoration.
2.  Recognition before repetition.
3.  Story before cliché.
4.  Contrast before visual noise.
5.  Consistency without monotony.
6.  Performance is part of design.
7.  Accessibility is a quality requirement.
8.  No visual claim should imply a product quality the product does not
    deliver.

## 1.6 Audience hypotheses to validate

Do not assume one audience segment explains every purchase. Research and
analytics should test segments such as: - Football-culture enthusiasts
seeking more design-led clothing - Streetwear buyers who want cultural
references without a conventional fan-shirt look - Gift buyers
purchasing around players, teams, tournaments, or milestones - Returning
customers collecting multiple drops or collections

For each segment, validate motivations, price sensitivity, size/fit
concerns, trust barriers, preferred channels, and repeat-purchase
behavior.

## 1.7 Brand architecture

Use a **masterbrand + collection system**.

-   **Masterbrand:** Triple Z identity, logo, core typography, design
    principles, ecommerce behavior, and quality bar.
-   **Collections:** Distinct stories and controlled visual variations
    under the masterbrand.
-   **Products:** Individual designs with clear naming, accurate
    specifications, and consistent product-information architecture.
-   **Campaigns:** Temporary art direction that can be more expressive
    without changing the permanent identity.

A collection may introduce its own palette and graphics, but it must not
invent a new logo system or unrelated typography without a documented
exception.

# 2. Creative Direction

## 2.1 Selected direction

**Direction B + C: Football Editorial + Signature Streetwear.**\
The result should be bold enough to have character, but controlled
enough to feel premium. It should not become loud, cluttered, overly
athletic, or dependent on visual effects.

### Football Editorial contributes

-   Narrative and cultural context
-   Strong editorial composition
-   Archival references and carefully used data-like typography
-   Cinematic but credible photography
-   Collection storytelling

### Signature Streetwear contributes

-   Ownable graphic codes
-   Strong product-level details
-   Confident typography
-   Unexpected but disciplined layouts
-   Repeatable visual elements derived from the Triple Z identity

### Premium restraint contributes

-   Clear hierarchy
-   Controlled color
-   Generous breathing room where it improves focus
-   High-quality image crops
-   Consistent alignment and spacing
-   Minimal use of ornamental effects

## 2.2 What this direction is not

-   Not a generic black-and-gold luxury template.
-   Not a football news website.
-   Not a replica of a club's official merchandise system.
-   Not a maximalist streetwear collage on every page.
-   Not a collection of animations that slow down shopping.
-   Not a design that relies on the logo being oversized everywhere.
-   Not a promise of premium quality unsupported by product reality.

## 2.3 Art-direction rules

1.  One dominant idea per composition.
2.  Product imagery must remain the primary focus in commerce contexts.
3.  Campaign imagery can be expressive; transactional UI must stay
    clear.
4.  Football references should be intentional and legible, not
    decorative filler.
5.  Use contrast, crop, typography, texture, and composition before
    adding effects.
6.  Keep collection-specific graphics within a defined system.
7.  Do not use fake grain, fake stitching, artificial fabric textures,
    or render effects to misrepresent the actual product.
8.  Do not use player likenesses, club marks, league marks, tournament
    marks, or protected kit designs in commercial materials without
    appropriate rights and approvals.

# 3. Logo System

## 3.1 Logo audit before finalization

The supplied logo features a Triple Z wordmark and a three-Z visual
device with dimensional blue treatment on a dark field. This is an
initial observation, not a final technical audit of the original vector
artwork.

Before production, obtain or create: - Editable vector master (SVG, AI,
or EPS) - Transparent PNG exports for approved use cases - Monochrome
version - Reversed/light version - Small-size digital version if
necessary - Embroidery/print production artwork - Clear-space and
minimum-size rules - Correct color specifications for digital and print

## 3.2 Logo variants

Proposed variant architecture: 1. **Primary lockup:** Main symbol +
wordmark. 2. **Symbol:** Three-Z device for small, constrained, or
repeat-pattern use. 3. **Wordmark:** Wordmark alone where the name must
be read clearly. 4. **Monochrome:** Single-color version for embroidery,
embossing, labels, and constrained production. 5. **Reversed:** Version
for dark or light backgrounds as required. 6. **Micro mark:** Only if
testing proves the primary symbol loses legibility at small sizes.

Do not create additional variants merely for novelty. Every variant must
solve a real application problem.

## 3.3 Logo rules

-   Preserve original proportions; never stretch, compress, skew, or
    rotate without explicit approval.
-   Maintain a defined clear space around the mark.
-   Do not add arbitrary outlines, glows, drop shadows, bevels, or
    gradients.
-   Do not place the logo on backgrounds that reduce contrast or obscure
    its shape.
-   Do not rebuild the logo by typing the name in a similar-looking
    font.
-   Use production-specific artwork for embroidery, screen printing,
    heat transfer, and engraving.
-   Test readability at the smallest real-world size, not only on a
    large presentation board.

## 3.4 Three-Z graphic device

The three-Z element is a potential proprietary visual asset. Explore its
use as: - A compact signature mark - A subtle repeat pattern - A layout
anchor or framing device - A small woven label or embroidery - A digital
transition or restrained motion motif - A collection identifier, only
where it does not confuse the masterbrand

The pattern must be derived from approved master artwork. Define scale,
spacing, density, and approved color combinations. Do not use it on
every surface by default.

## 3.5 Production gate

Before approval, test the logo on: - Dark and light backgrounds - Small
mobile header - Product card - Product-detail page - Woven label and
hangtag - Embroidery at realistic thread density - Single-color print -
Packaging - Social avatar

# 4. Color System

## 4.1 Color strategy

Separate **brand colors** from **collection colors** and **functional UI
colors**. The website must not inherit a collection's palette as its
permanent system.

The logo's current blue treatment and the product references suggest a
dark foundation may be appropriate, but final color decisions must be
made from original assets and real product photography, then tested for
contrast, consistency, print feasibility, and commercial use.

## 4.2 Proposed token architecture

The values below are intentionally not finalized. Replace the
provisional values after visual exploration and contrast testing.

  Token                       Purpose                 Status
  --------------------------- ----------------------- -----------------------

  `color.brand.secondary`     Secondary brand         Provisional
                              signature               

  `color.brand.accent`        Limited emphasis and    Provisional
                              signature detail        

  `color.surface.canvas`      Main page background    Provisional

  `color.surface.primary`     Primary component       Provisional
                              surface

                              card surface            

  `color.text.primary`        Main readable text      Must pass contrast

  `color.text.secondary`      Supporting text         Must pass contrast
                                                      tests

  `color.border.subtle`       Low-emphasis borders    Provisional

  `color.state.success`       Success states          Functional

  `color.state.warning`       Warning states          Functional

  `color.state.error`         Error states            Functional

  `color.state.info`          Informational states    Functional
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4.3 Palette rules

-   Limit the number of colors competing in one composition.
-   A collection palette may complement the brand palette but must not
    redefine the permanent brand.
-   Gold, metallic effects, or gradients are accents---not automatic
    symbols of premium quality.
-   Do not encode meaning by color alone; include text, icons, or other
    cues.
-   Functional states must remain distinguishable and accessible.
-   Validate colors on calibrated displays and with actual product
    imagery.
-   Print colors require separate proofing; screen HEX values are not
    print specifications.

## 4.4 Accessibility

Target WCAG 2.2 AA contrast: - Normal text: at least 4.5:1 - Large text:
at least 3:1 - Essential interface components and meaningful graphics:
at least 3:1 against adjacent colors where applicable

Do not reduce text contrast to achieve a fashionable muted look. Use
contrast testing on actual component states, including hover, focus,
disabled, error, and selected states.

# 5. Typography System

## 5.1 Typography goals

Typography should feel contemporary, confident, and editorial without
sacrificing legibility. It must support English brand expression,
product information, prices, size labels, navigation, and Arabic if the
store serves Arabic-speaking customers.

## 5.2 Font selection criteria

Evaluate candidate families against: - Distinctive but durable
character - Strong numerals for prices, sizes, and football references -
Clear small-size readability - Available weights and licensing -
Performance and file size - Latin and Arabic language coverage, if
needed - Consistency across desktop, mobile, and print

Do not select a font solely because it appears in a moodboard. Confirm
licensing for web embedding and commercial use.

## 5.3 Type roles

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Role                    Use                     Rule
  ----------------------- ----------------------- -----------------------
  Display                 Hero headlines and      Expressive, limited use
                          campaign statements     

  Heading                 Page and section titles Clear hierarchy

  Body                    Product descriptions    Readable and restrained
                          and editorial copy      

  UI                      Navigation, buttons,    High clarity
                          filters, form labels    

  Numeric                 Prices, sizes, product  Strong numeral
                          codes                   legibility

  Micro                   Labels, metadata,       Never too small to read
                          breadcrumbs             
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Use a modular scale as a starting point, then validate real layouts.
Avoid using too many weights, sizes, letter spacings, and text styles
without a defined purpose.

## 5.4 Typography behavior

-   Establish a consistent hierarchy for desktop and mobile.
-   Keep body copy comfortably readable.
-   Use uppercase selectively; long uppercase strings reduce
    readability.
-   Avoid excessive tracking on small labels.
-   Ensure price and discount hierarchy is clear without misleading
    emphasis.
-   Use tabular numerals where alignment benefits prices or product
    codes.
-   Define Arabic typography separately if Arabic is supported; test
    shaping, punctuation, numerals, and mixed-direction content.

# 6. Layout, Spacing, Shape, and Elevation

## 6.1 Layout principles

-   Build on a consistent responsive grid.
-   Keep content widths appropriate to their purpose; long product
    descriptions should not span excessively wide lines.
-   Align product cards, text blocks, and controls consistently.
-   Use asymmetry in editorial sections only when it improves the story
    or product focus.
-   Preserve predictable structure in checkout and account flows.

## 6.2 Spacing tokens

Use a documented spacing scale rather than arbitrary per-component
values. A provisional base scale may use multiples of 4 px, with larger
section spacing defined separately. Final tokens must be tested in
actual pages.

Example token names: - `space.1`, `space.2`, `space.3`, `space.4` -
`space.6`, `space.8`, `space.12`, `space.16` - `layout.page-gutter` -
`layout.section-gap` - `layout.content-max`

Avoid encoding layout decisions as one-off magic numbers when a token
can express the intent.

## 6.3 Shape language

Use a consistent radius system. Do not combine sharp editorial panels,
pill-shaped controls, and heavily rounded cards without a deliberate
hierarchy.

Proposed roles: - Small radius: compact controls and small surfaces -
Medium radius: cards and standard containers - Large radius: rare,
high-emphasis panels - Pill: only for elements whose behavior or brand
language justifies it

## 6.4 Borders and shadows

-   Prefer clear hierarchy and surface contrast over decorative shadows.
-   Use shadows sparingly and consistently.
-   Do not use glow effects as a substitute for hierarchy.
-   Define focus rings independently from decorative borders.
-   Ensure components remain clear in low-light and high-brightness
    environments.

# 7. Photography and Image System

## 7.1 Image roles

1.  **Campaign hero:** Brand story and collection atmosphere.
2.  **Product primary:** Accurate, clear view of the actual item.
3.  **Product detail:** Fabric, embroidery, seams, labels, print, and
    construction.
4.  **On-body / styling:** Fit, proportion, and real-world use.
5.  **Editorial story:** Football-culture context and collection
    narrative.
6.  **Commerce support:** Size guide, material, care, and detail
    visuals.

## 7.2 Product image standards

-   Show the actual product accurately.
-   Keep product color as faithful as reasonably possible.
-   Provide consistent angles and crop rules across a collection.
-   Include detail images where construction or texture is a purchase
    consideration.
-   Avoid aggressive color grading that changes the perceived item.
-   Do not use AI-generated product imagery to imply construction,
    details, or materials that do not exist in the sellable product.
-   Use accessible alternative text for meaningful product images.
-   Ensure the primary image communicates the item quickly at card size.

## 7.3 Art direction

Campaign images can use dramatic lighting, cinematic environments,
editorial crops, and cultural references. Product pages must prioritize
accurate representation and useful information.

The system should define: - Aspect ratios by placement - Safe areas for
text overlays - Crop behavior on mobile - Background and lighting
principles - Product scale in frame - Naming and export conventions -
Retouching boundaries - Rights and model releases

## 7.4 Image performance

-   Serve responsive image sizes.
-   Use modern formats where supported, with fallbacks as needed.
-   Avoid loading full-resolution campaign assets into small product
    cards.
-   Set image dimensions or aspect ratios to prevent layout shift.
-   Lazy-load below-the-fold imagery where appropriate; do not lazy-load
    the primary above-the-fold image if it harms LCP.
-   Measure real-user performance, not only local preview speed.

# 8. Motion and Interaction

## 8.1 Motion principle

Motion should communicate hierarchy, feedback, or continuity---not
merely prove that the website is animated.

## 8.2 Approved motion categories

-   Short hover/focus feedback
-   Subtle image transitions
-   Drawer and modal entry/exit
-   Cart feedback
-   Filter and sorting updates
-   Page-level transitions only when they preserve navigation clarity
    and performance

## 8.3 Motion rules

-   Use consistent durations and easing tokens.
-   Keep essential content available without waiting for animation.
-   Avoid scroll-jacking and forced cinematic intros.
-   Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.
-   Do not animate large areas unnecessarily.
-   Never let animation prevent rapid add-to-cart or checkout actions.
-   Test on lower-end mobile devices and reduced-bandwidth connections.

# 9. Digital Design Tokens

## 9.1 Token layers

Use three layers: 1. **Primitive tokens:** Raw values, such as color
values and spacing units. 2. **Semantic tokens:** Intent, such as
primary text, page surface, and interactive accent. 3. **Component
tokens:** Localized choices only when a component has a justified
special requirement.

Components should consume semantic tokens wherever possible. Avoid
hardcoding the same color or spacing across many files.

## 9.2 Naming conventions

Use consistent, purpose-led names. Example:

``` css
:root {
  /* Provisional examples: replace with approved brand values */
  --color-brand-primary: var(--primitive-brand-primary);
  --color-surface-page: var(--primitive-neutral-0);
  --color-surface-raised: var(--primitive-neutral-50);
  --color-text-primary: var(--primitive-neutral-950);
  --color-text-secondary: var(--primitive-neutral-650);
  --color-border-subtle: var(--primitive-neutral-200);

  --space-1: 0.25rem;
  --space-2: 0.5rem;
  --space-3: 0.75rem;
  --space-4: 1rem;
  --space-6: 1.5rem;
  --space-8: 2rem;

  --radius-sm: 0.25rem;
  --radius-md: 0.5rem;
  --radius-lg: 0.75rem;

  --focus-ring-width: 2px;
}
```

This is a structural example, not a final token file. Primitive values,
neutrals, and semantic mappings must be completed after the palette is
approved.

## 9.3 Governance

-   Tokens are the source of truth.
-   Components should not introduce undocumented brand colors.
-   New tokens require a clear use case.
-   Breaking changes must be documented.
-   Deprecated tokens must be removed after migration, not left
    indefinitely.
-   Keep design-tool tokens and code tokens synchronized through a
    documented workflow.

# 10. Ecommerce Information Architecture

## 10.1 Core page types

The final sitemap should reflect the actual business model and product
catalog. The following is a proposed baseline:

-   Home
-   Shop / All products
-   Collections
-   Collection landing page
-   Product detail
-   Search results
-   Cart
-   Checkout
-   Order confirmation
-   Customer account / order history, if supported
-   Size guide
-   Shipping and delivery
-   Returns and exchanges
-   FAQ / customer support
-   About / brand story
-   Contact
-   Legal and privacy pages

Do not create pages simply to fill a sitemap. Each page needs a defined
customer task and a clear owner for its content.

## 10.2 Navigation principles

-   Prioritize customer language over internal merchandising
    terminology.
-   Keep primary navigation focused.
-   Make search easy to find and useful.
-   Ensure collection discovery works on mobile.
-   Preserve a clear path from collection to product to cart.
-   Avoid hiding key shipping, sizing, or return information in
    inaccessible places.
-   Make promotional navigation useful, not an endless list of banners.

## 10.3 Home page hierarchy

Proposed hierarchy: 1. Announcement or service message, only when useful
2. Header and primary navigation 3. Hero campaign with a clear purpose
and action 4. Featured collection or current drop 5. Product discovery /
best sellers where supported by real data 6. Brand story or design point
of view 7. Product-detail or craft storytelling 8. Trust and service
information 9. Newsletter or customer relationship module, if justified
10. Footer with navigation, support, policies, and social links

The exact sequence must be validated against commercial goals and
customer behavior. Avoid stacking multiple competing hero banners.

## 10.4 Collection page

Must support: - Clear collection title and story - Product count when
useful - Sorting - Relevant filters - Selected-filter visibility - Clear
filter reset - Responsive grid - Loading, empty, and error states -
Pagination or progressive loading with a clear navigation strategy -
Shareable filter/search state where technically appropriate

## 10.5 Product detail page

Must answer, quickly and honestly: - What is the product? - What does it
look like from multiple angles? - What is the price? - What size should
I choose? - What is it made of? - How does it fit? - How do I care for
it? - Is it available? - When and how can it be delivered? - What are
the return/exchange conditions?

Recommended modules: - Image gallery with accessible controls - Product
name and concise description - Price and legitimate promotion details -
Color/variant selection, if applicable - Size selection and size guide -
Stock/availability messaging - Add-to-cart - Shipping and returns
summary - Material and care details - Fit information - Related
products, only when genuinely relevant - Reviews, only if authentic and
appropriately moderated

On mobile, keep purchase controls easy to reach without covering
essential content or creating a frustrating sticky panel.

## 10.6 Cart and checkout

-   Show item, selected variant, quantity, and price clearly.
-   Make quantity and removal controls accessible.
-   Disclose shipping, fees, and relevant conditions before final
    commitment.
-   Preserve cart state appropriately.
-   Avoid forced account creation unless there is a justified business
    requirement.
-   Provide clear validation errors and recovery paths.
-   Do not use deceptive urgency, false scarcity, preselected add-ons,
    or confusing opt-outs.
-   Track checkout funnel steps without collecting unnecessary personal
    data.

# 12. Accessibility and Inclusive Design

Accessibility is a release requirement, not an optional enhancement.

Target WCAG 2.2 AA and test manually as well as automatically.

Minimum requirements: - Keyboard access to all core journeys - Visible
focus indicators - Logical focus order - Correct headings and
landmarks - Form labels and clear error messages - Useful image
alternative text - Contrast-compliant text and controls - Touch targets
sized appropriately for mobile use - No color-only communication -
Reduced-motion support - Accessible dialogs, drawers, menus, and
galleries - Screen-reader announcement for cart updates and important
state changes - Zoom and reflow without loss of content or
functionality - Clear language and predictable navigation

Test at least one complete purchase journey with keyboard only and one
with a screen reader before release.

# 13. Performance and Technical Quality

Premium design must not come at the expense of a slow storefront.

## 13.1 Performance goals

Use Core Web Vitals as release targets, evaluated at the 75th percentile
of real-user data where available: - LCP: 2.5 seconds or less - INP: 200
milliseconds or less - CLS: 0.1 or less

These are targets, not a claim that the current or future site already
meets them.

## 13.2 Implementation principles

-   Load only the fonts and weights actually used.
-   Optimize and responsively size images.
-   Avoid oversized JavaScript bundles.
-   Prefer CSS for simple visual effects.
-   Avoid unnecessary client-side rendering for static content.
-   Prevent layout shifts by reserving media dimensions.
-   Keep third-party scripts limited and audited.
-   Avoid autoplay video with sound.
-   Ensure consent and analytics scripts do not block essential
    interactions.
-   Test realistic mobile hardware and network conditions.
-   Measure before optimizing; do not add complex caching or animation
    without evidence.

## 13.3 Quality gates

Before a release: - No critical console errors - No broken product
images or variant states - No dead-end checkout paths - No layout
overflow at supported widths - No major accessibility blockers - No
material performance regression against the baseline - No unapproved
logo, font, or color substitutions - No misleading product information -
No accidental exposure of private customer data

# 14. Trust, Content, and Commercial Integrity

The interface should make buying easier without manipulating customers.

-   Product images and descriptions must accurately reflect the item.
-   Discounts and original prices must be truthful and supportable.
-   Stock messages must reflect actual inventory.
-   Delivery estimates must be realistic.
-   Return and exchange terms must be accessible before purchase.
-   Reviews and testimonials must be authentic.
-   Do not fabricate press coverage, certifications, customer counts, or
    product claims.
-   Do not use countdown timers unless there is a genuine, enforced
    deadline.
-   Explain material, fit, care, and product limitations clearly.
-   Protect customer data and collect only what is needed.

For football-related commercial work, establish a rights review for
player likenesses, team and league marks, tournament names and logos,
archival imagery, photography, and kit designs. Brand inspiration does
not automatically grant commercial usage rights.

# 15. Content Design and Brand Voice

## 15.1 Voice attributes

-   Confident
-   Concise
-   Culturally aware
-   Specific
-   Human
-   Never artificially grandiose

## 15.2 Writing rules

-   Use short, informative product descriptions.
-   Lead with the product's actual differentiators.
-   Avoid unsupported superlatives such as "best in the world."
-   Explain design references without assuming every customer knows the
    history.
-   Keep UI language direct and consistent.
-   Separate campaign copy from functional instructions.
-   Maintain consistent product naming, collection naming, size
    terminology, and care information.

## 15.3 Product content model

Each product should have structured fields, as applicable: - Product
name - Collection - Short description - Full description - Price and
promotion fields - Available variants - Size and fit information -
Materials - Construction/details - Care instructions - Product imagery
and alt text - Stock/availability - Shipping and return information -
SKU or internal identifier - Relevant tags and related products

Do not bury essential sizing or care information inside marketing prose.

# 16. Collection System

## 16.1 Collection brief template

Every new collection should have a short approved brief: - Collection
name - Core idea and cultural context - Target customer / occasion -
Product list - Approved visual references and rights status - Collection
palette - Collection-specific graphic elements - Photography direction -
Product details and production constraints - Required ecommerce assets -
Launch content requirements - Success measures

## 16.2 Collection rules

-   Masterbrand typography and logo rules remain stable unless an
    exception is approved.
-   Collection colors should not conflict with product accuracy.
-   Collection graphics should have a clear concept and not be added as
    filler.
-   Create a complete asset set for product page, collection page,
    campaign, social, and paid placements.
-   Review the collection on the actual product and on the live site
    before launch.

## 16.3 Launch asset checklist

-   Hero campaign image/video
-   Collection thumbnail
-   Product primary and detail images
-   Mobile crops
-   Product descriptions and specifications
-   Size and fit details
-   Social launch assets
-   Paid-media variants
-   Email/SMS assets, if used
-   Metadata and social sharing images
-   Alt text
-   Rights approvals
-   Inventory and variant validation

# 17. Design Operations and Governance

## 17.1 Source of truth

Maintain one controlled source for: - Logo masters - Color and
typography tokens - Component library - Photography and export rules -
Approved templates - Collection briefs - Accessibility and QA
checklists - Change log

Avoid separate, conflicting copies of the same brand rules across teams.

## 17.2 Change control

Every significant change should record: - What changed - Why it
changed - Which components and channels are affected - Who approved it -
Whether migration work is required - How the change will be tested

## 17.3 Exceptions

Exceptions are allowed when they solve a real communication or product
problem. Document the rationale and scope. Do not let exceptions quietly
become a second design system.

## 17.4 Naming and asset conventions

Use consistent, searchable file names. Example:
`TZ_[collection]_[product-or-campaign]_[asset-role]_[ratio]_[version]`

Keep editable masters separate from exported production files. Include
dimensions, format, color profile, and rights/usage notes where
relevant.

# 18. Measurement and Iteration

Design decisions should be evaluated against customer outcomes, not only
internal aesthetic preference.

Potential measures: - Product-list click-through rate - Product-detail
engagement - Add-to-cart rate - Cart-to-checkout progression - Checkout
completion - Search usage and search exits - Filter usage and no-result
rate - Size-guide engagement - Return/exchange reasons related to fit or
product expectation - Repeat purchase and collection discovery -
Real-user Core Web Vitals - Accessibility defects and support contacts
caused by UX confusion

Do not optimize one metric in isolation. For example, a higher
add-to-cart rate is not a success if returns, cancellations, or customer
complaints rise because product information became less clear.

Use analytics with appropriate consent, data minimization, and privacy
controls. Establish a baseline before claiming a redesign improved
performance.

# 19. Implementation Roadmap

## Phase 1 --- Audit and inputs

**Deliverables** - Original logo assets and formats audit - Product and
collection inventory - Current visual-asset review - Ecommerce goals and
key customer journeys - Rights and production constraints - Existing
technical requirements, if known

**Exit gate:** The team agrees on the business and customer problems the
system must solve.

## Phase 2 --- Creative direction

**Deliverables** - One coherent visual direction based on Editorial
Football + Signature Streetwear - Reference board with rationale - Logo
usage exploration - Typography candidates - Color hypotheses -
Photography and graphic-language principles - A small set of
real-product applications

**Exit gate:** The direction works on actual products and digital
contexts, not only on presentation slides.

## Phase 3 --- Identity and tokens

**Deliverables** - Approved logo system - Final color tokens and
contrast report - Typography system and font licenses - Spacing, grid,
radius, border, elevation, and motion tokens - Three-Z graphic device
rules - Asset/export specifications

**Exit gate:** Values and rules are documented and reproducible.

## Phase 4 --- Ecommerce UX and component system

**Deliverables** - Sitemap and key journeys - Responsive page
templates - Component inventory and states - Product/collection content
model - Accessibility behavior - Design-to-development handoff notes

**Exit gate:** Core shopping journeys are testable on mobile and
desktop.

## Phase 5 --- Prototype and validation

**Deliverables** - Clickable prototype - Usability tests for discovery,
sizing, product evaluation, and checkout - Contrast and keyboard
testing - Responsive QA - Performance budget and measurement plan

**Exit gate:** Critical usability and accessibility issues are resolved
or explicitly accepted with a plan.

## Phase 6 --- Build and launch

**Deliverables** - Implemented tokens and components - Content and
product asset validation - Analytics instrumentation - SEO metadata and
structured-data validation - Cross-browser and device QA - Release
checklist and rollback plan

**Exit gate:** No critical blockers remain, and key metrics are
measurable.

## Phase 7 --- Governance and optimization

**Deliverables** - Design-system maintenance process - Collection launch
template - Quarterly review of components and tokens - UX and
performance monitoring - Documented experiments and decisions

**Exit gate:** Teams can add new collections without inventing
inconsistent patterns.

# 20. Acceptance Criteria

The system is not complete merely because the screens look polished. It
is ready for production when:

## Brand

-   Logo variants and use rules are documented.
-   The identity is distinguishable from generic sportswear and generic
    luxury templates.
-   The identity works across multiple collection palettes.
-   Graphic elements are ownable, consistent, and technically
    producible.
-   All commercial imagery and marks have the required permissions.

## Digital system

-   Tokens are named, documented, and implemented consistently.
-   Components include states and responsive behavior.
-   Product, collection, cart, and checkout patterns are specified.
-   No critical path depends on hover-only behavior.
-   Arabic/RTL is fully tested if supported.
-   Accessibility and performance targets have been tested.

## Commerce

-   Product details, sizing, materials, availability, shipping, and
    returns are clear.
-   Product imagery is accurate and responsive.
-   Cart and checkout flows handle errors and recovery.
-   Analytics events are defined and privacy-reviewed.
-   Promotions and urgency messaging are truthful.

## Operations

-   One source of truth exists.
-   Owners and approval responsibilities are defined.
-   Asset and token changes are documented.
-   New collections can be launched within the system without
    redesigning the masterbrand.

# 21. Open Decisions --- Do Not Guess

The following must be confirmed during discovery rather than invented: -
Primary market(s), languages, and currency requirements - Actual
ecommerce platform and technical stack - Current logo source files and
brand ownership/usage constraints - Product catalog, variants, sizing
system, and inventory model - Shipping, returns, payment, and
customer-support policies - Existing customer research and analytics -
Final font licenses and brand palette - Which football-related assets
are owned, licensed, or unavailable for commercial use - Print,
embroidery, packaging, and supplier constraints - Accessibility and
performance baseline - Internal approval owner and design-system
maintainer

# 22. Final Creative Standard

Triple Z should feel like a confident fashion brand with a meaningful
relationship to football---not a football shop trying to look premium,
and not a generic streetwear label borrowing football graphics.

The website should express the brand's world, make the products
desirable, answer practical buying questions, and remove friction from
the purchase. The physical product, imagery, copy, packaging, and
digital interface must tell the same story.

**The standard is not "Does it look expensive?" The standard is: "Is it
distinctive, credible, usable, consistent, producible, and commercially
effective?"**

That is the basis on which every future Triple Z design decision should
be judged.
